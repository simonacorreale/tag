Research plan — Redesigning grocery delivery

Master file for the research. One week, 2026-10-10 to 2026-10-16: research questions, desk research, 3 interviews, 2 observations. The brief is in brief.md. The map of all files is in RADEME.md [link target not found: RADEME.md].

Goal

Design one feature that improves the online grocery delivery experience of a supermarket chain such as Esselunga. The basket is big, weekly and perishable. The feature must address one moment only: building the basket, handling substitutions, or receiving the order.

By the end of the week the research must say which moment to design for and which problem inside it is worth solving.

Interview notes — P1 Federica Franco

Profilo partecipante

  • Nome / ID: P1 Federica Franco
  • Nucleo familiare: 2 persone (lei e il partner Michele).
  • Contesto: Precedentemente residente a Milano senza auto (alta dipendenza dalla spesa online); attualmente con auto, frequenza online ridotta ma ancora presente.

Abitudini di acquisto e servizi utilizzati

  • Frequenza: In passato a Milano 2–3 volte al mese; spese di grandi dimensioni (anche fino a ~350€).
  • Piattaforme:
    • Esselunga online: servizio principale per carrelli grandi/stock, sfruttando promozioni (es. soglie per spedizione gratuita). Nota negativa: impossibilità di usare buoni pasto in app.
    • Glovo: utilizzato saltuariamente per offerte e possibilità di pagare con buoni pasto.
  • Driver di utilizzo:
    • Acquisto di prodotti pesanti/ingombranti in stock (latte, bibite, detersivi) per evitare il trasporto a piedi.
    • Risparmio di tempo ed evitamento del supermercato affollato nel weekend dopo la settimana lavorativa.

Articoli ricorrenti e reperibilità

  • Sempre presenti nel carrello: Prodotti a marchio del distributore (linea Esselunga Smart: salumi, formaggio grattugiato, farina).
  • Reperibilità: Generalmente buona; il disservizio principale riguarda le novità commerciali (es. nuovi gelati) che arrivano online con mesi di ritardo rispetto al lancio.

Gestione out-of-stock e sostituzioni (Pain Point Principale)

  • Frustrazione sulle sostituzioni forzate: Obbligo di accettare sostituzioni generiche o dover deselezionare opzioni; stress legato alle differenze di prezzo e ai rimborsi.
  • Categorie non sostituibili:
    • Prodotti premium / brand specifici legati al gusto personale (es. Coca-Cola vs Pepsi, Nutella vs sottomarca/private label). In questi casi preferisce lo storno/mancata consegna rispetto a un'alternativa non gradita.
  • Categorie sostituibili: Beni generici o di base, dove il brand non incide sull'esperienza d'uso.

Scelta dello slot e logistica di consegna

  • Fasce orarie: Difficoltà a trovare lo slot desiderato (es. venerdì pomeriggio). Necessità di pianificare e inoltrare l'ordine con 2–3 giorni di anticipo per garantirsi la fascia.
  • Esperienza di consegna: Molto positiva. Puntualità rispettata, imballaggio accurato (bottiglie in vetro protette con pluriball) e corrieri disponibili a portare i carichi al piano.

Prodotti freschi

  • Comportamento: Scetticismo verso i freschi sfusi da pesare (preferisce sceglierli di persona al banco).
  • Compromesso online: Acquisto limitato a prodotti freschi già confezionati e pre-pesati (es. carote in busta, insalate pronte). Nessun problema riscontrato su qualità e scadenze.

Opportunità di miglioramento emerse (Feature & UX)

  1. Sincronizzazione scorte in tempo reale: Aggiornare la disponibilità in app con lo stock effettivo del punto vendita di riferimento per evitare rotture di stock a monte.
  2. Politica di rimborso trasparente: Storno immediato del prodotto mancante senza dover attendere rimborsi successivi.
  3. Riprogettazione UI carrello e catalogo: Migliorare la leggibilità dell'interfaccia (layout più pulito) e semplificare l'inserimento quantità per i prodotti freschi.

P1 Federica Franco — 2026-10-10

Profile: P1 Federica Franco, 30. Shops for herself and her partner. Used Esselunga online 2 to 3 times a month when she lived in Milan without a car. She now has a car and orders online less often. Video call.

Coverage: the whole guide, in two recordings (questions 1 to 9, then 11 to 22). Questions 7, 10, 12, 14, 17 and 18 were not asked; where another answer touches them, this is noted below. Most answers describe her past habit in Milan, not a recent order, and she does not remember which item was last substituted.

Last order in brief: a bulk order of heavy items — milk, drinks, detergents — made from the app because she was in Milan without a car. She checked for brand promotions that give free delivery before ordering.

Building the basket

  • Observations:
    • She orders online for two reasons: tiredness after the working week, and heavy items she cannot carry on foot.
    • Orders were large, "around 350" (she does not say the unit; probably euros).
    • Before a big order she looks for brand promotions that give free delivery.
    • A core of the basket is always the same: Esselunga's Smart own-brand line (cured meats, grated cheese, flour). She still buys these in store.
    • The assortment is wide enough. What is missing are newly launched products, which arrive months late.
    • The Esselunga app does not accept meal vouchers. That is why she used Glovo a couple of times.
    • She buys little fresh produce or meat online, because she prefers to choose it herself in store. When she did, she chose pre-packed items, not ones sold by weight.
    • She finds the app uncomfortable to shop in: products are hard to see on the page, and setting the quantity for loose fruit and vegetables was confusing.
  • Quotes:
    • "It is really uncomfortable to shop from the apps, the products are hard to see on the pages."
    • "When you have to buy fresh fruit and vegetables and you had to enter the quantity, it was really awkward, you could not understand anything."
    • "After a week of work I did not want to go to the supermarket at the weekend as well, which is even worse."
    • "We definitely always bought Esselunga's Smart brand."
    • "If you see a product that has just come out, you do not find it straight away. You have to wait a few more months."

Substitutions

  • Observations:
    • She raised substitutions herself, unprompted, as the hardest part of ordering (question 5).
    • When the brand she chose was unavailable, she felt she had no choice but to accept Esselunga's basic own-brand product instead.
    • She mentions the price difference, but it is not clear who paid it or in which direction.
    • She describes it as a stress that came back with every order.
    • To place an order she had to tick a box authorising the service to substitute unavailable products. She describes it as mandatory, with no way to refuse.
    • She does not remember which item was last substituted. The service left free samples in the order, which she read as an apology.
    • Her reaction was calm. For most items, the same product from another brand was fine. For missing items she contacted Esselunga and was refunded.
    • A few items are not negotiable: Coca-Cola (any Coca-Cola variant is fine, Pepsi is not) and Nutella. Her rule: items she pays a lot for, or where she cares about the taste.
    • For those items she would rather not receive the product and have the price taken off than receive a substitute.
    • This qualifies her answer to question 5: the stress is tied to having no say, and to the few items she cares about, not to substitutions in general.
  • Quotes:
    • "You had to tick the box saying they were authorised to substitute your products if they were not available."
    • "When they replaced the product with the same thing from a different brand, I did not mind, because I am not too fussy about these things."
    • "Coca-Cola and Pepsi are two completely separate worlds for me."
    • "If I pay a lot for it, I do not accept that you substitute it. I would rather you leave it out and take the price off what I paid."
    • "The hardest part in general, or anyway the part that gets on your nerves, was that if that brand was not there, you had to accept the basic Esselunga one as a substitute, paying the price difference of course."
    • "That is the stress I had every time I did a shop."

Receiving the order

  • Observations:
    • Getting the delivery window she wanted was hard. She ordered two or three days ahead, or more, to secure it; otherwise she took whatever was left.
    • Once booked, the window was always respected.
    • No problems at delivery. Packaging was careful, and couriers were kind and carried the order up to her door.
    • The pre-packed fresh products she ordered were always good, for both quality and expiry date. She thinks she was lucky: she knows people who were not, and says it depends on who picks the order.
  • Quotes:
    • "Very often it is hard to get the window you want. Either I ordered early or I never got the window I wanted, so I had to make do with the ones available."
    • "When they give you a window, they always respect it."
    • "I think I was also lucky, because I know people who did not have a good experience. And it depends a lot on who does your shopping."

Most painful moment, in the participant's words: substitutions. "That is the stress I had every time I did a shop."

One thing they would change: first, limit substitutions by keeping the app's stock aligned with the store the order is picked from, and removing items from the app as they run out. Second, the layout of the app, which she finds uncomfortable.

Hypotheses:

  • H1 — supported. She accepts another brand for most items and names two she will not compromise on, Coca-Cola and Nutella.
  • H2 — not supported as worded. She describes a single mandatory tick box, with no item-by-item preferences to set. Her problem is the absence of control, not the effort of using it.
  • H3 — mixed. For most items a different brand is fine. For the few she cares about, only the exact product is acceptable.
  • H4 — no evidence. She did not say how or when she found out about a substitution.

Interviewer notes:

  • She regularly buys Esselunga's own-brand Smart line by choice, yet dislikes receiving an own-brand product as a substitute. The problem may be the lack of choice, not the own brand itself. This is an interpretation.
  • Her proposed fix is upstream of substitution: accurate stock in the app, so the unavailable item cannot be ordered at all. She also dislikes paying first and waiting for a refund.
  • Friction is concentrated before delivery: the app layout, the substitution consent, booking a window. The delivery itself was positive on every point she mentioned.
  • Still unclear: what "paying the price difference" means, and how and when she found out about a substitution.
  • Her description of the mandatory tick box is her recollection of the Esselunga app at the time. It is not verified against the current app.
  • Topics outside the three moments: promotions as a reason to order, and meal vouchers as a reason to use another service.
  • She suggested two questions for the next interviews: how accurately the order was packed, and how one supermarket's service compares with another's.

  • Nome / ID: P2 Luca Volpi
  • Nucleo familiare: 4 persone (lui, la moglie e due figli piccoli).
  • Contesto: Dipendente UniCredit a Milano, con sconto aziendale su Esselunga. Di solito la spesa online la fa la moglie; l'ultima l'ha fatta lui. Smart working non pianificabile in anticipo.

Abitudini di acquisto e servizi utilizzati

  • Frequenza: Circa una volta al mese, in modo irregolare; nessuna routine fissa (giorno o carrello ricorrente).
  • Copertura: Spesa completa online, dalla lunga conservazione ai freschi (frutta e verdura incluse). Nessuna divisione tra cosa comprare online e cosa in negozio.
  • Piattaforme:
    • Esselunga online: servizio usato quasi sempre negli ultimi anni.
    • Carrefour, Coop e un terzo servizio che non ricorda: provati in passato, con esperienze negative (freschi meno freschi).
  • Driver di scelta:
    • Sconto per i dipendenti UniCredit: la sensazione di "perdere qualcosa" se cambiasse servizio.
    • Esselunga percepito come lo standard a Milano, quindi più veloce e più fresco.
    • Risparmio di tempo: con due bambini piccoli non è possibile girare più negozi.

Composizione del carrello (Pain Point Principale)

  • Ricerca: La ricerca restituisce troppi risultati non pertinenti (cercando "arancia" escono succo e cioccolato all'arancia); bisogna raffinare a mano.
  • Assortimento troppo ampio: Scorrere "quattro pagine di detersivi" per sceglierne uno è la parte più faticosa.
  • Prodotto non trovato: Per articoli particolari (es. succo di limone) non capisce se ha sbagliato termine o se il prodotto non esiste.
  • Scelta guidata dalle promozioni: Poco legato al marchio; sceglie in base all'offerta della settimana. Apprezza gli abbinamenti suggeriti (pasta + sugo pronto).
  • Ordinamento per prezzo al chilo: All'inizio spiazzante, poi apprezzato come "il vero prezzo".
  • Tempo: Oltre 20 minuti in un'unica sessione.
  • Carrello ricorrente: Di fatto nessuno. Esiste un nucleo fisso (pane, latte, acqua, uova) ma non hanno mai salvato un carrello.

Esperimento con assistente AI (Gemini)

  • Input: Lista della spesa condivisa su Alexa (aggiornata da tutta la famiglia) + menu settimanale per i bambini + volantino promozioni Esselunga (richiesto da Gemini).
  • Output: Carrello proposto incrociando lista, menu e promozioni; poi rifinitura manuale. Gemini ha anche suggerito come ottimizzare il carrello finale.
  • Valutazione: Molto positiva; ha ridotto molto la fatica. Prima volta, il sabato precedente all'intervista.

Sostituzioni e problemi di qualità

  • Sostituzioni: Spesso non si accorge nemmeno di aver ricevuto un prodotto diverso.
  • Problemi reali: Prodotti mancanti o di bassa qualità (es. banane spaccate).
  • Reclamo: Molto semplice: foto in app e rimborso immediato. Lo fa per principio anche per importi di un euro.
  • Nessuna categoria esclusa dall'online: Si definisce "disilluso" rispetto a chi vuole pannolini di marca o la verdura del fruttivendolo.

Scelta dello slot e logistica di consegna

  • Fasce orarie: Scelte in base agli impegni familiari; sorpreso di trovare libera la fascia 18–20 per il giorno dopo, di solito poco disponibile.
  • Giorno della consegna: Nessuno in casa; ha risposto al citofono smart dal telefono e la moglie è arrivata in tempo. Consegna al piano, prodotti corretti.
  • Problemi storici: Nessuno che ricordi.

Prodotti freschi

  • Percezione: I freschi di Esselunga a Casa sono più freschi di quelli del supermercato (non stanno in esposizione) e migliori di quelli degli altri servizi provati.

Opportunità di miglioramento emerse (Feature & UX)

  1. Consegna senza presenza: Locker refrigerato o accesso in casa, per non dover garantire giorni prima di essere a casa in una fascia.
  2. Carrelli salvati e pacchetti: Carrelli standard salvabili o pacchetti tematici (es. colazione con 3–4 prodotti).
  3. Bundle promozionali: Sconto (es. 30%) se si acquistano più prodotti insieme, in alternativa all'aggiunta di un prodotto alla volta.
  4. Ricerca più precisa: Risultati pertinenti e chiarezza su quando un prodotto non è disponibile.

Signals

  • Shared pain point, building the basket: P2 names search and choice overload as the hardest part (Q5), where P1 named substitutions. Building the basket was P1's second friction point (app layout, Q21 in notes/2026-10-10-interview-note-federica-franco-p1.md).
  • Divergence from P1, substitutions: P2 often does not notice a substitute at all and names no protected items. His friction after delivery is missing or damaged items, solved by the in-app claim.
  • Shared pain point, delivery windows: both P1 and P2 must commit days ahead to a window. P1 solves it by ordering early; P2 cannot plan his presence at home and asks for unattended delivery.
  • Shared observation, freshness: P1 and P2 are both satisfied with Esselunga fresh products. P2 attributes it to stock not being on display, which fits the desk competitor table's claim that Esselunga a Casa controls freshness from a central warehouse.
  • New theme: P2 built his basket with a general-purpose AI assistant from a shared household list, a weekly menu and the promotions leaflet. Not covered by the guide or by hypotheses H1 to H4.

Q2. How do you do your main grocery shop today? How much of it is online? Q4. Tell me about the last time you ordered groceries online. "Usually my wife does the shopping, but last time I did it, because she had absolutely no time even to sit at the computer. I did it in a particularly curious way: I wanted to try doing it with Gemini, not Gemini Enterprise because it was outside work [unclear], but I have Gemini for personal use, and I asked it explicitly to do the shopping with me, so I let it guide me. We have a shared shopping list on Alexa, so whoever notices something is missing adds it to Alexa, and we also have a weekly menu, which we don't follow 100%, to give the children a varied distribution of foods. Direct chain with its own online service. The only service both interview participants use, so the only one where desk claims can be checked against shoppers. Main result

On paper, Esselunga covers the three moments well: an upfront substitution choice, 2-hour windows, refrigerated vans. The interviews confirm the delivery and the fresh quality, but contradict the substitution control (P1) and show friction the desk research does not cover: search (P2) and booking a window in advance (P1, P2).

Desk research — Redesigning grocery delivery

Secondary evidence on the three moments named in the brief: building the basket, handling substitutions, receiving the order. The desk research part of the research plan.

Scope: online grocery delivery by supermarket chains in general. Findings about a single chain are labelled with its name.

How to read this file

  • Each finding has an ID, a source reference (S1, S2, ...) and a confidence level.
  • Market says where the data comes from. Most of it is not Italian.
  • Findings marked to verify were read in search summaries or press coverage and have not yet been checked against the original source.

Summary

  1. Substitutions are the best documented pain point of online grocery. They are frequent, often unsatisfying, and the interfaces for controlling them are hard to use.
  2. Shoppers accept substitutions in principle but want control over specific items.
  3. Evidence on delivery windows and on receiving the order is thin in this pass. It is a gap, not a sign that those moments work well.
  4. Almost nothing found so far describes how Italian supermarket chains handle unavailable items. The substitution evidence comes from the UK and the US.

Implications for the design

  • A substitution feature should ask less, not more: a global default plus exceptions for the items the shopper cares about (D7, D11).
  • Purchase history is an underused basis for choosing substitutes (D12, D13).
  • Dietary and household constraints must limit what can be substituted (D15).
  • The time of notice matters: before delivery the shopper can still decide, at the door they can only accept or refuse (D16, D18, D19).

These are hypotheses to test in the interviews, not conclusions.

Moment with the most friction: handling substitutions, for P1. She named it unprompted as the hardest part (Q5) and again as the first thing she would change (Q21). Building the basket comes second, because of the app layout. Receiving the order was positive apart from booking the window.

Insights to carry into the moment selection on 2026-10-16:

  1. The problem is control, not substitution itself. P1 is relaxed about most swaps and upset only where she had no say.
  2. A short list of protected items would cover her need: a few products, defined by price and taste, that must never be substituted.
  3. For protected items the acceptable outcome is "leave it out and do not charge me", not a better substitute.
  4. Prevention ranks above repair for her: stock shown in the app should match the store that picks the order.
  5. One gap remains for all three interviews: how and when the shopper learns about a substitution. Ask question 12 explicitly to P2 and P3.

Research note structure — instructions for AI agents

This file is the format specification for this research repository. Any AI agent — or human — creating or editing a note here follows it exactly. Consistency between notes is what makes the knowledge base retrievable later: the same fields, in the same order, in every file.

The metadata block

Every note must open with a YAML frontmatter block, before any content:

---
title: "Descriptive, specific — one line"
date: 2026-10-08            # when the evidence was collected (YYYY-MM-DD)
time: "07:55–08:20"         # optional — time of day for interviews and observations
activity: interview         # interview | observation | shadowing | mystery-shopping | desk-research | plan
source: "Who or what produced the evidence — a person and their role, or a publication"
author: "Who collected or produced the note"
channel: in-person          # in-person | phone | video | web | publication
method: semi-structured interview   # the specific method used
location: Milano Centrale   # where — omit for desk research
tags: [commute, delays]     # lowercase, consistent vocabulary, no invention
confidence: high            # high | medium | low — how much you trust this evidence
grade: primary              # primary | secondary | tertiary — the source grade
status: complete            # draft | complete | needs-review
related: [plan.md]          # links to related notes in this repository
---

Field rules:

  • confidence is about this piece of evidence, not the topic. A low-confidence note is an honest note — mark it, don't hide it.
  • grade follows the source ladder: primary (you collected it, or it is original data) > secondary (someone's reporting of data) > tertiary (opinion, blog, unverified).
  • tags come from the research plan's tag list whenever possible. Do not invent near-synonyms (delay vs delays) — one term, one tag.
  • date is the collection date, not the writing date. The writing history is in git.

File naming

YYYY-MM-DD-type-source.md

Example: 2026-10-08-interview-marta-bianchi.md — lowercase, hyphen-separated. The filename alone says when it was collected, what kind of material it is, and where it came from.

Body structure

The body is plain prose organized into units: each ## heading is one unit, and one unit is one complete meaning. Rules:

  1. One unit, one meaning. If a unit covers two ideas, split it into two units.
  2. Self-contained sentences. Anyone — human or agent — reading only this note must understand it without opening other files.
  3. Quotes carry timestamps. In interview and observation notes, quotes are blockquotes with the timestamp from the transcript: > "..." — [00:04:12].
  4. No interpretation mixed with evidence. A separate final unit (## Signals) maps what was said to the brief's pain points — clearly marked as reading, not as what the person said.

Folders

research/ plan.md ← the research plan — the container everything answers to brief.md ← project brief and requirements index.md ← the map: every note listed with type and status notes/ ← derived, structured notes (one file per source) all-results/ ← processed research findings (desk and interview results) raw/ ← unprocessed material: recordings, spontaneous notes, screenshots raw-docs/ ← original raw documents and source materials desk/ ← raw desk research materials interview/ ← interview scripts and raw transcripts

Non-negotiable rules

  1. One file per source. Never merge, never rewrite. New evidence is a new note.
  2. Never edit a transcript. The transcript is the artifact; the note is the derived view. If a finding is questioned later, you go back to the transcript.
  3. Every note lands in index.md the moment it is committed, with type and status.
  4. Update related links whenever a note connects to another — the network is the scalability.
  5. Same metadata at 10 notes and at 200. Nothing about the shape changes as the base grows.
